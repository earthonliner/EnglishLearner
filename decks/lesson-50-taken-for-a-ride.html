No. "Oh dear… I forgot to put you off." – the conductor forgot, and the bus had reached the end of the line.
Q2 · thinkWhose mistake was it – and how did the writer turn the mistake to his advantage?
The conductor's. But instead of getting off at the wrong place, the writer stays on the bus and gets a free ride all the way back – so he really was "taken for a ride", in both senses.

Grammar focus

"I'll tell you where to get off."

Question word + to-infinitive = a shorter way to say a question-word clause with should.

The machine

I'll tell you where you should get off.⟶I'll tell you where to get off.
wh-word+subject+should+verb⟶wh-word + to + verb
Works after know, tell, ask, show, decide, wonder, forget… Same subject on both sides, or the subject is "you/anyone". Not with why. Also in the text: I don't know where it is – here the clause has a real subject (it), so it stays a clause.

Click the parts you can cut

Core: —
① Cut the opening participle phrase Looking round ② Cut the adverbial with a shock ③ What's left is the trunk: I realized (that) I was the only passenger. "left on the bus" describes passenger – it can go too.
Then rebuild: Looking round + with a shock are the "flavour" a good writer adds.

Why is the last line funny?

💬 Discuss (3 min)

"Well, in that case, I prefer to stay on the bus."
  • He has just been carried past his stop – and now he chooses to be carried all the way back. Is he making the best of it, or being lazy?
  • Back to the title: was he taken for a ride in meaning 1, meaning 2, or both?
  • What would YOU have done at the end of the line?
